HEART-SOUND NOISE RESEARCH AND NEXT IMPLEMENTATION
Reviewed November 14, 2026. Research only: no new denoiser installed or tested.

FINDING

The current app needs better noise handling and more robust beat identification.
A narrower bandpass alone is unlikely to solve its movement sensitivity. No
source reviewed demonstrates guaranteed recovery of every heartbeat from all
background sounds and phone-on-skin contact artifacts.

The current estimator uses a 25–160 Hz bandpass, an RMS envelope, autocorrelation,
and an alternating-peak heuristic. It has no learned audio denoiser or motion
input. One unacceptable candidate-sound gap rejects the cycle window; a missed
or extra peak can disturb the alternating labels. These are current-source facts.
They do not establish which rejected windows contain true usable heartbeats.

In the latest controlled private test, the original repeating-pattern check
accepted every warmed update while the cycle check rejected a substantial
subset. This supports investigating the peak/sequence logic as well as cleaning
the sound. Private counts and measurements remain in diagnostic-logs.

PRIMARY SOURCES CHECKED

1. Echoes iPhone study (2022)
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9708017/
https://doi.org/10.1093/ehjdh/ztac044

The study assessed phone-recorded heart-sound quality in non-medical users.
Over 80% obtained at least one recording judged good quality. This is not a BPM
accuracy rate, a murmur-detection result, or evidence of immunity to movement.
Guidance and retries were part of the approach. No complete denoising
implementation was established from this paper during this review.

2. FonoCheck hospital smartphone study (2025)
https://academic.oup.com/ehjdh/article/6/3/486/8006140
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12088729/
https://doi.org/10.1093/ehjdh/ztaf007

Phone recordings were assessed in hospitalized participants. Conversation,
breathing, equipment and movement remained noise sources. A 20–150 Hz bandpass
was used in assessment; our BPM filter is already similar in range. The authors
explicitly distinguish recognizable first/second heart sounds from demonstrating
murmur capture. Their limitations discuss the loss of relevant higher-frequency
murmur information under a narrow assessment filter. Thus copying that cutoff
would not establish a noise-robust or murmur-capable phone pipeline.

3. LU-Net heart-sound denoising (2023 paper and author repository)
https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/10173517/
https://github.com/ShamsNafisaAli/LU-Net-Heart-Sound-Denoising-

The paper describes learned suppression of lung/ambient noise and evaluates
synthetic noise mixtures plus real noisy PASCAL recordings. Those conditions
must not be presented as validation of phone-on-skin rubbing or our iPhone app.

Repository tree inspected at commit:
ffb0d6b7ad901ea5e6fe9140a6f935b6a4231fe4
The author repository actually lists pretrained LU-Net, U-Net and FCN .h5 files.
LU-Net.h5 is 16,100,512 bytes. This review verified file metadata, not a successful
model download or execution. Codes/config.py specifies 800-sample / 0.8-second
windows at 1000 Hz. Codes/model.py implements a convolutional encoder/decoder
with bidirectional LSTM branches. Python/Keras execution, preprocessing fidelity,
iPhone conversion, latency and behavior on our captures remain untested.

The model.py header declares MIT licensing; the inspected tree has no standalone
license file and the binary weights have no separate terms established here.
Verify the intended source/weight redistribution terms before bundling. No
external code or trained weights were copied into this project in this review.

The 1 kHz input also limits retained frequency information to below 500 Hz.
Consequently, do not silently use this output as the sole input for a future
murmur classifier. Keep a separate wider-band original path and validate the
actual clinical target on its own appropriate data.

4. Schmidt spike removal / Springer heart-sound segmentation
https://physionet.org/content/hss/1.0/
https://github.com/davidspringer/Springer-Segmentation-Code/blob/master/schmidt_spike_removal.m

The source explicitly detects unusually large transients in short audio windows
relative to a robust amplitude reference and removes the local transient. The
package also supplies a trained sequence-model approach to heart-sound states.
This provides concrete reference methods for isolated clicks and sound timing;
neither guarantees recovery during sustained rubbing or lost contact. The
reference implementation is MATLAB and GPL-3.0. No implementation was copied.

RECOMMENDED BUILD ORDER — ENGINEERING PROPOSALS, NOT MEASURED IMPROVEMENTS

A. Make contamination local rather than poisoning the whole window.
   Detect brief abnormal transients in the waveform before envelope extraction.
   Keep an explicit time mask for contaminated sections. Exclude masked pairs
   from correlation and avoid timing a beat interval across a corrupted gap.
   Preserve real timestamps: concatenating clean fragments would change BPM.
   Require enough clean cycles and current evidence; long corruption stays unsure.

B. Replace fragile alternating-peak logic.
   Evaluate a robust sequence/beat tracker that can label extra peaks as noise
   and handle a missing candidate, rather than assigning every detected sound
   alternately forever. Compare against the current method and the published
   sequence-model baseline. Do not just remove the existing rejection checks.

C. Benchmark a learned heart-specific denoiser.
   Run the author LU-Net pipeline on a Mac against public licensed heart sounds
   with controlled noise. Compare it with the current bandpass, transient
   suppression and a conventional adaptive/spectral baseline. Confirm source
   preprocessing and chunk-boundary behavior before drawing conclusions. Convert
   to an iPhone model only if it preserves target information and improves the
   actual measurement task at an acceptable runtime cost.

D. Evaluate phone-motion input as an additional quality cue.
   Core Motion can provide processed movement information:
   https://developer.apple.com/documentation/coremotion/getting-processed-device-motion-data
   Test whether it identifies handling artifacts usefully. It is not an ambient
   noise microphone and cannot by itself reconstruct missing heart audio. Its
   benefit for this app remains a hypothesis.

E. Define success with a noise benchmark before another tuning session.
   Separate conditions: quiet, steady fan/hum, speech, short taps, rubbing,
   brief repositioning, and lost contact. Use measured clean public recordings
   and documented synthetic mixtures first; label synthetic contact noise as a
   simulation rather than real movement validation. Split source people and
   recordings before making variations, so derivatives do not leak across sets.
   Measure BPM difference, returned/unsure coverage, false beats on noise-only
   inputs, and time to recover after a transient. For the murmur model, also
   check false negatives/positives and preservation of murmur-relevant details.
   A cleaner-sounding output or an unchanged number on screen is insufficient.

PRACTICAL LIMITS

One microphone receives a mixture, and movement/contact noise can overlap the
heart signal. Fully buried or clipped information cannot be guaranteed recoverable
from that one mixture; a model output is an estimate, not proof of the hidden
waveform. Heart-like artifacts and altered/removed abnormal sounds are failure
cases to test explicitly.

The existing private logs store derived features, not original audio. They can
evaluate the current feature-level counters but cannot establish that a new raw
audio denoiser works. Start with public audio; any later personal audio retention
should be explicitly opt-in, local, separate from logs and off by default. No
personal recordings were requested or collected by this research task.
